**A 1958 Les Paul can be a factory Goldtop or a factory sunburst. Establish which one you have before comparing specifications, prices or photographs.** Gibson changed the Standard's finish during the year, after introducing humbucking pickups in 1957. That makes 1958 the link between the PAF Goldtop and the sunburst guitars collectors call Bursts.

The familiar 1958 combination is a single-cutaway mahogany body with a carved maple top, two PAF humbuckers, a rounded neck and relatively narrow original frets. They don't tell you whether a particular guitar retains its factory finish, pickups or neck shape.

This guide uses a **2011 R8 reissue from our shop** to illustrate the visible layout. It isn't a vintage 1958 guitar. If you're identifying an inherited instrument or preparing to [sell a Gibson Les Paul](/sell-my-gibson-guitar/), start with clear photographs and its ownership history before cleaning it or changing parts.

## The 1958 Transition

The sunburst finish arrived around the middle of 1958. Before that change, Gibson was making the gold-finished Les Paul with humbuckers that had developed during the previous year. The [1957 Les Paul Goldtop guide](/post/1957-les-paul-goldtop-guide/) explains the immediate predecessor, including the move away from P-90 pickups.

Sunburst exposed the maple top beneath a shaded finish. It made the grain, figure and joining of the wood part of the guitar's appearance. A gold finish concealed much of that information. The switch changed what a buyer saw more than it changed the basic pickup-and-bridge arrangement.

Gibson's own [1958 Historic model history](https://images.gibson.com/Products/Electric-Guitars/2018/Custom/58-LP-Standard/Press/Documents/58-LP-Standard-One-Sheet.pdf) identifies 1958 as the year the sunburst Standard replaced the gold-finished model. Its specification table describes a modern reissue, however. Don't transfer every item in a current product sheet to an original guitar.

For a concrete period example, Gibson documented a [1958 Goldtop with serial number 8 3313](https://www.gibson.com/products/gibson-certified-vintage-1958-gibson-les-paul-goldtop-darkback-goldtop) in its Certified Vintage program. A year digit beginning with 8 therefore doesn't establish that a guitar left the factory in sunburst. The finish history still needs examination.

### Were All 1958 Les Paul Standards Sunbursts?

No. [Goldtops](/post/1957-les-paul-goldtop-guide/) belong to 1958 too, because Gibson changed the finish during the year. A gold-finished 1958 Les Paul with humbuckers can be period-correct. The separate question is whether the finish on the individual guitar is original. Compare it with the model's construction, component dates and repair history before deciding what it is.

## Reading The R8 Photo

The guitar at the top of this page was listed by our shop as a **2011 Gibson R8 Les Paul Standard 1958 Reissue**. Its visible top has broad areas of relatively plain grain rather than the prominent stripes shown on the R9 in our [1959 Les Paul Standard guide](/post/1959-gibson-les-paul-standard-guide/).

Look at the two separate metal assemblies below the bridge pickup. The adjustable bridge supports the strings; the stopbar anchors them. The cream pickguard sits beside the pickups, and the four gold knobs control the two pickup circuits. Those are useful features to learn from this photograph without making a claim about the age of any part.

The listing also described a figured back and recorded a weight of 8.9 pounds. Those details belong to this particular 2011 reissue. They aren't a weight standard or wood-selection rule for original 1958 guitars. The back figure also demonstrates why “plain top” only describes the top: it doesn't mean every surface of that instrument has plain grain.

Our [pictured R9 in the 1959 Standard guide](/post/1959-gibson-les-paul-standard-guide/#what-joes-r9-photo-shows) was also listed at 8.9 pounds. Two different reissue models, with visibly different tops, had the same reported weight. That's a useful reason to ask for an individual guitar's weight instead of assuming the R8 or R9 designation tells you how heavy it will be.

A front photograph can't show the neck's depth, the undersides of the pickups, pot codes or hidden repair work. It also can't establish whether the frets match a factory specification. Use the image to understand the layout, then gather the views needed to answer the questions it leaves open.

### Does R8 Mean The Guitar Was Made In 1958?

R8 identifies a modern 1958-style Historic reissue, not its manufacturing year. Our example was made in 2011. A reissue can reproduce vintage-style lettering, hardware and serial-number conventions. Establish its actual production year and model from its documentation and the instrument together, rather than treating the reference year in its name as a date of manufacture.

## Neck And Frets

The substantial neck is one of the reasons players seek a 1958 Standard. The usual description is a full, rounded profile, but “1958 neck” isn't one universal measurement. Two guitars can have different shoulders and feel different even when their measured depths are close.

Original 1958 fretwire is generally associated with the narrower specification that preceded the wider wire commonly found on 1959 Standards. Gibson discusses the narrow-fret distinction in its [1958 reissue description](https://www.gibson.com/products/gibson-custom-1958-les-paul-standard-reissue-vos-bourbon-burst). That modern model is a reference for the intended design, not proof that an older R8 or a repaired vintage guitar has the same wire.

On a guitar that has been played for decades, fret condition may matter more to the player than the original specification. Ask whether the frets have been replaced, how much usable height remains, and whether the fingerboard was planed during the work. Look along the neck for unevenness and have a repairer check relief, truss-rod function and available adjustment.

A refret and a reshaped neck are different changes. Replacement frets address a wearing surface. A neck shave removes wood and changes the profile itself. The paperwork should describe each separately, including any associated finish work. “It plays great” doesn't answer either originality question.

### Does A Refret Make A 1958 Les Paul A Bad Guitar?

A good refret can make a worn guitar much easier to play. For a collector, it still changes the [originality](/gibson-les-paul-dating-guide/) description and should be disclosed. Check the quality of the work, the remaining fingerboard thickness and any binding repairs. Compare a refretted example with other refretted guitars, rather than assuming it belongs in the same condition category as an untouched one.

## PAF Pickups And Controls

PAF stands for “Patent Applied For.” Seth Lover designed Gibson's original humbucker, and the pickups reached Les Pauls during 1957. A 1958 Standard therefore belongs to the early humbucker period; it wasn't the year Gibson first introduced the pickup.

The design uses two coils to reduce hum picked up from the surrounding electrical environment. Players often seek these pickups for their response to picking strength and the guitar's volume control. There isn't one resistance reading or recorded sound that identifies every original PAF. Pickup height, pots, strings, amplifier settings and later work all affect what you hear.

A proper pickup description separates several questions: Is it a period PAF? Are its coils intact? Has it been rewound? Are the magnet and cover original to that pickup? Is there evidence that the pickup has remained in this guitar? A correct-looking decal answers none of those questions by itself. Our [PAF and patent-number pickup guide](/post/gibson-paf-patent-number-pickup-guide/) explains the broader identification problem.

The controls deserve the same attention. Each pickup has a volume and tone control, selected through the three-way switch. Period-style pots and capacitors can be installed later, so the appraisal should consider the components, wiring and repair evidence together.

Pot codes identify the component's maker and production date. For example, the manufacturer prefix 134 refers to Centralab and 137 to CTS. The code dates the pot, not the completed guitar. Our [Gibson pot-code guide](/gibson-pot-codes/) shows how to read the markings without turning a replaced component into a false build date.

### Should I Remove Pickup Covers To Check The Guitar?

Leave soldered covers and wiring intact for the initial assessment. Removing a cover can disturb evidence of earlier work and create a repair that wasn't needed. Start with exterior photographs and any existing service records. If pickup removal or internal inspection is necessary, have a qualified repairer document the work and preserve every original part.

## Hardware And Plastics

The usual Burst arrangement combines an ABR-1 bridge with a separate stopbar tailpiece. A stopbar is not the same component as the one-piece wraparound bridge used on earlier Les Pauls. This distinction matters when an older Goldtop has been modified to resemble a later guitar.

On a claimed 1958 Standard, examine the bridge mounting, saddles, plating and evidence of changed hardware. A later bridge can be a practical repair without changing the age of the body. It does change an “all-original” description. Factory vibrato examples and later vibrato installations also need individual documentation rather than an assumption based on visible hardware alone.

Kluson tuner details are another cross-check. The line count stamped into the metal gear housing and the ring count molded into the plastic button are separate features. The [Gibson tuner dating guide](/gibson-tuner-dating-guide/) explains those distinctions and why replaced tuners don't date a guitar.

Gold bonnet or top-hat knobs, cream pickup rings, a cream pickguard and an amber-colored switch tip form the familiar appearance. Inspect each as a separate part. Age, wear or a molded marking alone doesn't establish that a plastic part was installed at the factory. Reproductions can resemble period parts closely, and original parts can have been transferred between guitars.

Use the [Gibson knob chronology](/gibson-knob-dating-guide/) for the transition to later reflector styles, and the [physical features and hardware guide](/gibson-physical-features-hardware-guide/) for the wider comparison. These references are most useful when the components disagree and you need to decide which question to investigate next.

## Serial Numbers And Originality

A typical original 1958 solidbody serial is ink-stamped on the back of the headstock and begins with an 8. Record the complete number, including its spacing, and photograph it without enhancing or retracing the markings. A familiar number format is a starting point, not a certificate.

Modern reissues are a particular reason to be cautious: their numbering and appearance intentionally recall older instruments. The [Gibson serial-number guide](/how-to-read-gibson-serial-numbers/) and [Les Paul dating guide](/gibson-les-paul-dating-guide/) help place the serial alongside the construction and component evidence.

For 1958, ask the finish question separately from the age question. A period Gibson can have a later sunburst refinish. An older Goldtop can have been converted to humbuckers and later hardware. Neither situation becomes an original factory Burst simply because some of the wood or parts are from the 1950s.

An inspection should describe the top, back, neck, headstock and cavities individually. Check for headstock and heel repairs, changed tuner holes, filled hardware holes, routing changes, replaced binding and touch-up around repaired areas. Finish checking or yellowing alone doesn't settle the question, particularly when modern instruments can be deliberately aged.

### Can Photos Confirm An Original Factory Burst?

Photos can reveal inconsistencies and help plan an inspection, but they can't settle every question about finish, repairs or component history. A useful initial opinion should identify what the images show and what remains unverified. For a purchase or sale that depends on factory-original status, arrange an in-person examination before treating that status as established.

## 1958 Value Factors

An original sunburst Standard and a 1958 PAF Goldtop need different comparables. A refinished Goldtop in sunburst needs a different description again. Establishing that category is more useful than starting with the highest advertised Burst price you can find.

Within the correct category, document the following separately:

1. **Finish History:** Original finish, local touch-up, overspray and complete refinish aren't interchangeable descriptions.
2. **Structural Condition:** Headstock, heel and body repairs need clear photographs and repair records where available.
3. **Pickups And Electronics:** Identify replaced or repaired pickups and controls without hiding them under “mostly original.”
4. **Neck And Frets:** Record refrets, fingerboard work, neck reshaping and playing condition.
5. **Appearance And Provenance:** Describe the top and surviving color, then separate documented ownership from an unsupported story.
6. **Case And Accessories:** Include the case, receipts and retained parts, while checking whether they belong to the guitar.

The [vintage Les Paul market-value guide](/vintage-gibson-les-paul-market-value-guide/) provides the broader comparison. An asking price isn't a confirmed sale, and one unusually figured or celebrity-owned instrument isn't a reliable comparison for every 1958 Standard. Any estimate is specific to the evidence available; condition findings and market changes can alter it.

## 1958 Vs. 1959 Vs. 1960 Les Paul Standards

1958 introduces the sunburst finish and is associated with the fuller neck and narrower original frets. The [1959 Standard](/post/1959-gibson-les-paul-standard-guide/) continues the basic construction and is commonly associated with wider frets. The [1960 Standard](/post/1960-gibson-les-paul-standard-guide/) needs closer attention to changes within the year, including neck profiles, knobs and tuner buttons.

Don't reduce that comparison to plain top versus flame top. The pictured R8 is a plain-top reissue and the pictured R9 has strong figure, but those two modern guitars aren't a census of vintage production. Nor does a thicker neck automatically make a guitar better for every player. Compare the actual instrument, its condition and how it feels in your hands.

### How Many 1958 Bursts Were Made?

Treat an exact sunburst-only total cautiously. Shipping totals for a model and calendar year don't automatically isolate a finish introduced during that year. The Goldtop overlap is especially relevant to 1958. Our guide to [Gibson shipping totals and their limits](/post/gibson-shipping-totals-1948-1979/) explains why a ledger count needs context before it becomes a production claim.

## Prepare Your Guitar For Review

Start with photographs of the complete front and back, both sides of the headstock, the serial number, the neck heel and any damage. Add close views of the bridge, pickups, controls and tuner backs. Photograph the case and paperwork separately so an appraiser can distinguish evidence about the instrument from evidence about its accessories.

Write down what you know: who owned it, when it entered the family, who worked on it and which parts were changed. Keep uncertainty in the account. “My father bought it used around 1970” is more useful than converting that memory into an unsupported claim that he was the original owner.

Leave polishing, finish repair, pickup disassembly and parts replacement until the assessment is complete. If you have a loose original part, label and preserve it.

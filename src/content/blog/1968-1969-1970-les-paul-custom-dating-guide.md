<p class="norlin-lede"><strong>Dating a 1968, 1969 or 1970 Les Paul Custom takes a combination of clues. Early 1969 guitars can resemble 1968, while late 1969 can overlap 1970. Body layers, neck joints, headstock details and original component dates help separate them when the serial number can't.</strong></p>

<h2 id="dating-method">How To Date A 1968, 1969 Or 1970 Les Paul Custom</h2>

<p>The difficulty is that Gibson changed these guitars gradually. The 1968 Custom starts with a one-piece mahogany back and neck. During 1969, the body layers, neck assembly and neck joint change at different points. By 1970, the regular construction combines a pancake body and a three-piece mahogany neck.</p>
<p>Gibson also reused serial-number ranges. A lookup can return more than one year, and several construction features occur in adjacent years. The most convincing date is the one supported by the original features together, rather than a single number or part.</p>
<p>The explanations and photographs below show what each clue means, followed by a side-by-side comparison and worked examples. You can also <a href="/free-appraisal/">send photos for help dating your Custom</a>.</p>
<p>If you're still identifying the model, the <a href="/gibson-les-paul-dating-guide/">Gibson Les Paul dating guide</a> explains how the Custom differs from the Standard, Deluxe and other Les Pauls.</p>



<h2 id="body-and-neck-construction">One-Piece And Pancake Les Paul Custom Bodies And Necks</h2>

<p>The regular Customs in this comparison have a carved maple top, called a cap, over a mahogany back. A 1968 or early-1969 guitar has a one-piece mahogany back. That is the part meant by the usual shorthand one-piece body; the maple top is still separate.</p>
<p>Some early-1969 bodies add a thin maple sheet directly beneath the cap while retaining the full one-piece back. This upper crossbanding appears around the 535xxx to 537xxx portion of the observed early serial group, with each x representing another digit.</p>
<p>On a true <strong>pancake body</strong>, the thin maple sheet is farther down, between two thick mahogany sections. The carved maple cap sits above that layered back. This construction appears late in 1969 and is the regular pattern in 1970.</p>
<p>The position of the thin layer separates the two designs. A line around the side may help locate it, but black lacquer can hide the wood. A side-by-side join in the back is a different type of seam. The <a href="/gibson-physical-features-hardware-guide/#body-construction">Gibson body construction guide</a> illustrates the layer arrangement.</p>
<h3>How The Neck Construction Changes</h3>
<p>The neck changes on a separate schedule. A one-piece neck has one main piece of mahogany in its shaft. A three-piece neck has three long pieces joined side by side. The joins run along the neck, while the ebony fingerboard is attached to the front of either construction.</p>
<p>One-piece mahogany necks continue into spring 1969; three-piece mahogany necks are documented by May. A three-piece neck can therefore be paired with a one-piece back before the pancake body appears. The <a href="/gibson-physical-features-hardware-guide/#three-piece-mahogany-neck">neck construction photographs</a> show the lengthwise joins.</p>
<h3>What The Control-Cavity Shape Means</h3>
<p>The control cavity is the recess beneath the rear plastic cover, where the volume and tone controls sit. The early shape has relatively straight walls and a flat wooden floor. During early 1969, a shape with a small internal shelf or stepped ledge near the bottom appears.</p>
<p>That difference concerns the wood cutout around the electronics. It can support the early construction sequence, but isn't an exact month code. It's useful when existing cavity photographs show the shape clearly enough to compare.</p>

<h2 id="neck-tenons">1968 To 1970 Les Paul Custom Long And Transitional Tenons</h2>

<p>The long tenon is found on 1968 and early 1969 Customs. A shorter transitional joint appears around late April and May 1969, with overlapping neck and body assemblies. Sellers sometimes call this later form a short tenon, so the name is best accompanied by a photograph.</p>

<h2 id="volute-and-headstock">Les Paul Custom Volutes, Gibson Logos And Made In USA Stamps</h2>

<h3>No Volute, Small Volute And Later Volute Shapes</h3>
<p>A <strong>volute</strong> is a raised area of wood behind the nut, where the neck meets the headstock. The nut is the slotted piece supporting the strings before the tuners. A 1968 or early-1969 neck flows smoothly through this area without a volute.</p>
<p>A small swelling appears late in 1969 and can continue into early 1970. Later Customs can have a more pronounced ridge. The photographs compare a smooth neck with a larger volute on a 1974 Custom; the latter shows the shape clearly, rather than representing the small late-1969 version.</p>

<h3>Made In USA Stamp</h3>
<p>Factory 1968 and 1969 Customs lack a Made in USA stamp. Gibson introduced it during 1970, and early 1970 examples can also be unstamped. An original stamp sets a 1970-or-later limit; the absence of one doesn't prove a pre-1970 date.</p>
<h3>Dotted And Dotless Gibson Logos</h3>
<p>Earlier 1968 Customs can have the small dot above the i in Gibson. The usual late-1968 through 1971 logo lacks it, and the dot returns around 1972. The styles overlap on some guitars, so this is a pattern to compare with the rest of the instrument rather than a universal cutoff.</p>

<h2 id="tuners-and-hardware">1968 To 1970 Les Paul Custom Tuners, Bridges And Pickups</h2>

<p>The gold Kluson Super tuners commonly found on early Customs are called wafflebacks because of the ribbed pattern on their rear housings. Their buttons have a metal keystone shape. The rear-headstock photo below shows this family on a 1970 Custom.</p>
<p>Grover Rotomatics have round enclosed housings, as seen on the later headstock used in the volute comparison above. Some 1968 Customs had factory Grovers, and Grovers were also fitted later as upgrades. Extra holes or old washer impressions can help identify a change. The <a href="/gibson-tuner-dating-guide/#waffleback-tuners">Gibson tuner guide</a> compares the families and their mounting details.</p>
<p>The regular bridge arrangement is an ABR-1 Tune-O-Matic and separate stopbar. The bridge supports the strings on adjustable saddles, while the stopbar behind it anchors their ends. This layout is shared across the three years. The <a href="/gibson-physical-features-hardware-guide/#wired-abr-1">ABR-1 guide</a> shows the narrow bridge and its small posts.</p>
<p>The regular Custom also has two humbuckers. Patent Number describes a label on the metal underside of a pickup; T-Top describes a molded T on the plastic bobbin holding its coil. The same pickup can have both features. The photographed 1969 example below shows the patent label while its bobbins remain under the cover. The <a href="/post/gibson-paf-patent-number-pickup-guide/">Gibson pickup guide</a> explains these markings.</p>

<h2 id="serials-and-pot-codes">Gibson Serial Numbers And Pot Codes For 1968 To 1970</h2>

<p>The serial is impressed into the back of the headstock. Gibson reused six-digit ranges during this period, so one number can produce several possible years in a lookup. These guitars predate the later eight-digit date code. The <a href="/how-to-read-gibson-serial-numbers/">Gibson serial number guide</a> explains which system applies.</p>
<p>The earliest 501xxx to 505xxx Les Paul group belongs to the 1968 return. The 506xxx to 561xxx group crosses into 1969, followed by later 565xxx, 566xxx and 800xxx groups. Very late 891xxx to 897xxx and some 9xxxxx groups extend toward 1970. Each x represents another digit. These are observed Les Paul groups, not a complete Custom-only decoder.</p>
<h3>What A Pot Code Dates</h3>
<p>The volume and tone controls are potentiometers, usually called pots. Their metal casings can carry a manufacturer's production code, visible inside the rear control cavity. A common CTS format is <strong>137YYWW</strong>: 137 identifies CTS, YY gives the year and WW the week.</p>
<p>For example, 1376837 means week 37 of 1968, 1376902 means week 2 of 1969, and 1377014 means week 14 of 1970. These are examples of component dates, not guaranteed guitar assembly dates.</p>
<p>In the photograph below, 13769 is visible on the upper pot while solder partly hides the week digits. That gives an incomplete 1969 code without requiring a guess at the obscured numbers. Other markings can indicate electrical resistance or a part number.</p>
<p>If the pot is original, the guitar must have been assembled after that component was made. A 1968 pot can carry into 1969, and a 1969 pot can carry into 1970. A confirmed original 1970 pot rules out 1969 assembly. Replacements date later work instead, so the <a href="/gibson-pot-codes/#control-cavity-check">pot-code and control-cavity guide</a> also considers the wiring and solder joints.</p>

<h2 id="dating-examples">How To Weigh Conflicting 1968 To 1970 Dating Clues</h2>

<p>Consider an early-style Custom with a one-piece back and neck, long tenon, smooth headstock transition and original 1968 pots. That combination supports the early construction period, but the pot date alone can’t exclude assembly in early 1969. A shipping record or strong contemporary documentation may narrow the result.</p><p>Now consider a three-piece mahogany neck on a one-piece back with no Made in USA stamp. It fits the 1969 transition better than a simple all-features-changed-at-once timeline. The neck changed before the true pancake body became the later pattern.</p><p>A pancake back, laminated mahogany neck and small volute can leave late 1969 and early 1970 difficult to separate. An original Made in USA stamp or original 1970 pot sets a 1970-or-later limit. Absence of the stamp can still fit early 1970. Replacement pots or altered headstock finish remove some of that evidence.</p><p>When the surviving evidence supports late 1969 to early 1970, retain that range. A dated sales receipt records a sale, and a family photograph shows that the guitar existed by then; neither automatically records the assembly date. State the strongest supported conclusion instead of forcing a single year.</p>



<h2 id="photos-and-value">Photos And Condition Details For A Dating Appraisal</h2>

<p>Photograph the full front and back, both sides of the headstock, the serial and stamp area, the side profile behind the nut, the body edge, tuners, bridge posts and accessible pot markings. Include existing pickup-cavity pictures and any repair records. You don’t need to dismantle the guitar before sending a first set of photos.</p><p>Separate evidence that dates original construction from changes made during repairs. A refret affects the playing feel, a replaced pickup affects originality, and a headstock repair can obscure important contours and markings. One altered feature need not erase every unaffected clue.</p><p>After identifying the construction and the best-supported date, compare condition through the <a href="/vintage-gibson-les-paul-market-value-guide/">Les Paul market value guide</a>. The <a href="/post/norlin-les-paul-custom-year-guide/">Norlin-era Custom guide</a> carries the later timeline, variants and period prices.</p>

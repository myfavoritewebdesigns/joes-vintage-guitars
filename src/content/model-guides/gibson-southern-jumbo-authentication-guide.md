The Southern Jumbo is the dressed-up member of the J-45 family, with split parallelogram fingerboard markers and more elaborate trim. Both round-shoulder and later square-shoulder versions exist. “SJ” in this guide means Southern Jumbo, not SJ-200.

## Southern Jumbo Versus J-45, J-50 And Country Western

The split markers are a useful distinction from the dot-inlaid J-45 and J-50. Natural-finish SJ-family guitars lead into the SJN/Country Western story, but finish alone cannot identify one. The [J-45/J-50/SJ family guide](/post/identify-vintage-gibson-j45-j50-sj/) shows the broader comparison.

For a documented factory-era comparison, the [1966 Gibson catalog](https://www.vintageguitarandbass.com/gibson/catalogues/1966_29.php) presents the SJ and natural SJN together. It should be used for that period, not to judge a wartime guitar’s appointments.

## Wartime, Postwar And Square-Shoulder Clues

The SJ began during the 1940s wartime period. Banner-era construction can vary, including top wood and unusual body-wood configurations. A Banner emblem is one clue; it cannot independently prove a year, wood species or complete originality.

Postwar changes include the move from script to block headstock lettering, evolving guards and bridges, and eventual square shoulders. The source price guide places the body transition in 1962; specialist histories commonly place square shoulders in 1963. Treat 1962 to 1963 as a transition to investigate through the actual guitar and its records. [GuitarHQ’s SJ reference](https://guitarhq.com/sj.html) is useful for comparing these features.

Later SJ Deluxe guitars form another category. A later unbound fingerboard should be considered alongside the rest of that guitar’s construction rather than rejected because a 1950s SJ had binding.

This example makes the distinction concrete. Its present appearance records later work. The original model and the current finish need separate findings. Natural SJ production is documented in the mid-1950s, preceding the Country Western name; see [Folkway’s examined 1954 SJ Natural](https://folkwaymusic.com/museum/gibson-guitars/1954-gibson-sj-nat-0119).

Inspect the finish around the heel, binding, soundhole and bridge. A stripped sunburst can lose its most obvious visual clue. Do not infer factory originality merely because the exposed wood looks attractive.

## Numbers, Neck And Interior Evidence

Record any factory order number inside the body and any serial number on the headstock. Preserve faint letters, spaces and suffixes in the photograph instead of transcribing only the digits. The [Gibson number guide](/how-to-read-gibson-serial-numbers/) explains why the number must be checked against physical specifications.

Look at the neck joint, bridge plate and bracing for replaced wood, excess glue, additional holes or repair cleats. Ask about a neck reset and inspect its fit. Do not attempt to remove a neck, bridge or guard to date the guitar.

## Original Instrument, Repaired Instrument Or Reissue?

A sound repair does not turn an SJ into a fake. It changes the originality and condition description. Record the repair, the parts involved and whether it appears stable. Reissue names can include an old year such as “1942” while the guitar itself was built recently; establish the manufacturing date from the instrument and documentation.

## Photos To Send For Authentication

Send front and back views, headstock front and back, factory order number or serial, fingerboard binding and inlays, bridge and saddle, neck heel, and clear interior photographs if accessible. Include every known repair and any old receipts or family photographs.

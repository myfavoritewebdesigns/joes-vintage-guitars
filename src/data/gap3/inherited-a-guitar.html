If you've inherited a guitar, you don't have to decide quickly whether to keep it or sell it. Find out what it is, establish what kind of valuation the situation needs, and give the family room to decide what happens next.

For the instrument itself, start with looking after a guitar you have just found. That covers handling, storage and photographs. Here I'll focus on the questions an heir or executor faces after the guitar is safely accounted for.

If the practical questions are easier than the decision about letting it go, my page on keeping or selling a loved one's guitar addresses that separately. You can gather information without making a commitment to sell.

Whose Guitar Is It Right Now?

During an estate administration, an executor or administrator generally manages estate property until it is distributed. Being a beneficiary or having the guitar in your house doesn't necessarily mean you can sell it yet. The applicable law, ownership arrangements and estate documents can change the answer.

Ask the executor or estate's attorney who has authority over this particular instrument and who can authorize a sale. I'm a guitar dealer, not a lawyer or accountant, and I can't decide ownership from photographs. An identification or market opinion isn't permission to dispose of estate property.

You can still begin by making the situation clear when you ask about the guitar. Tell me if you're gathering information for an executor, trying to identify a family instrument or considering a sale after distribution. Those are different conversations, even when the same person is holding the case.

It can help the family to separate who is looking after the instrument from who is making decisions about it. Someone may have the best place to keep it while another person handles the estate paperwork. Those roles don't have to be settled by whoever first recognizes the brand on the headstock.

If relatives remember different promises about the guitar, put that question to the person handling the estate. A valuation can't settle a disagreement over what someone intended. Keeping those questions separate lets the guitar assessment remain useful without pretending it answers everything.

Which Kind Of Valuation Do You Need?

People use the word appraisal for several different requests. Before commissioning a report, find out who will use it and what they need it to establish. A family deciding whether to keep an instrument may need different information from an insurer or an estate's tax preparer.

A current market opinion can help a family understand what it has. It doesn't automatically serve as a signed report for official use. Likewise, an offer tells you what a particular buyer proposes, not what every possible buyer would pay or what an insurer would recognize.

An insurance figure shouldn't be treated as a promise of sale proceeds. Replacing a specific instrument through an appropriate seller is a different task from arranging a sale of the instrument you already own. Ask which definition the report uses before comparing it with an offer.

The first questions for the executor are practical: who requested the valuation, what is it for, what date must it reflect, and does it need to be a signed written report? If an attorney, insurer or accountant supplied requirements, share those requirements with the person preparing the appraisal.

It also helps to ask whether the request concerns one guitar or an entire collection. An informal answer about a recognizable instrument doesn't establish the contents or condition of several closed cases. The scope of the request should be clear before anyone assumes the information is complete.

Don't feel that you have to learn appraisal terminology to start. Saying that the executor needs a report, or that you only want to identify your father's guitar, gives me a useful starting point. We can establish what I can provide before you commission anything.

The Valuation Date Can Differ From Today

For federal tax basis, inherited property is generally valued at fair market value as of the owner's date of death, with exceptions and possible alternate valuation rules. A present-day opinion may therefore answer a different question from the one the estate needs. Ask the estate's tax preparer which date and documentation apply; I can't choose the tax treatment for your circumstances.

Identify It Before Comparing Listings

A familiar brand name doesn't identify a model or a production period. Makers often reuse model names, and later instruments can resemble much older ones. A listing that looks similar from the front may be a poor comparison for the guitar in your case.

If the instrument's identity is uncertain, finding out which guitar you have is the starting reference. There is no need to decide that it is valuable before asking. A plain identification can be useful even when the family intends to keep it.

The separate guides to recognizing the brand and working out the guitar's age explain the limits of names and numbers. Let the available evidence establish the identity instead of working backward from the most attractive listing.

A family description such as Dad's old Gibson can be completely accurate while still leaving the model unresolved. Keep the family name for it if you like, and let the identification add detail. You aren't expected to know a collector's vocabulary to look after an instrument.

When someone supplies an identification, ask what remains uncertain. A photograph can show a model feature while leaving a hidden repair or replaced part unresolved. A useful opinion should make those limits understandable rather than turn every early observation into a final conclusion.

Several Heirs, One Guitar

A guitar can't be divided physically in the way a set of books can. Families sometimes discuss one person keeping it while other estate assets are used to balance the distribution, selling it and dividing the proceeds, or keeping it within the family under an agreed arrangement. The executor or attorney can explain which options are available in the estate.

Before discussing those options, make room for anyone who has a particular attachment to the instrument. The person who plays may have a practical use for it, while another relative may associate it with a parent or grandparent. Those are different kinds of interest, and neither is measured by a market appraisal.

A shared written account of the identification and condition can give everyone the same information. That is easier to discuss than separate recollections of what someone thought a dealer said. Keep the distinction between a tentative opinion and a completed report visible to everyone using it.

If the family chooses to keep the guitar, a note of who has it and what the family agreed can prevent practical confusion later. That note isn't a substitute for estate documents or legal advice. It can simply keep the everyday arrangement understandable to the people involved.

Some families aren't ready to settle the long-term plan while dealing with the rest of a home. Ask the person handling the estate what timing is actually required. A buyer's availability should not be mistaken for the family's deadline.

If It Was A Musician's Guitar

A working musician may have replaced frets, fitted different tuners or changed pickups. Those choices often reflect years of use and maintenance. You don't need to be embarrassed if the instrument isn't exactly as it left the factory.

A refret means replacing the metal frets on the neck. Tuners are the mechanisms that tighten the strings, and pickups are the electrical components beneath an electric guitar's strings. Knowing those terms can help you understand a repair invoice without having to interpret the work yourself.

Changes do affect which guitars make useful comparisons. My guide to originality, repairs and guitar value explains the distinctions. The assessment should describe the instrument you have, including its use and repairs, without treating ordinary playing history as a family mistake.

If your relative kept old parts, keep their connection to the guitar clear. An invoice or a note from the owner may explain why a set was changed. Where there is no documentation, say that the parts came with it rather than declaring them original.

The family's memories and the instrument's mechanical history can sit alongside each other. A repair may have kept it in use for years. You don't have to choose between preserving that account and accurately describing the current condition.

Paperwork Worth Looking For

Original receipts, repair invoices, letters, photographs and the paper items kept in a case can help document an instrument. Case candy is the informal name for things such as tags, instructions and other material supplied with a guitar. Keep what belongs together rather than deciding in advance what a buyer will consider useful.

A receipt can connect a name, date and instrument. A repair invoice can explain a changed part or a later finish. A photograph of the owner playing it may show an earlier appearance, though the photograph alone doesn't prove every component stayed unchanged afterward.

Write down the memories that still have a known source. Distinguish something a relative remembers directly from a story passed along without a date. That preserves the family account without making it sound more certain than it is.

The original-owner and family guitar stories show the kinds of connections that can stay with an instrument. They aren't a required standard of documentation. A guitar can be assessed even when its early receipt or complete ownership history has been lost.

Personal papers may contain information the family doesn't want shared publicly. You can describe what you have before deciding what belongs in a sale description. Keeping the story with the guitar doesn't require publishing every private document.

Selling It, If The Family Decides To

Once the authorized person and the family have decided to consider a sale, the condition and identity give the discussion a concrete starting point. An offer can then be assessed as a proposed transaction. It shouldn't be used to settle ownership or substitute for a report requested for another purpose.

For several instruments, my guitar collection page and comparison of collection-selling options cover that larger task. The guide to online selling options addresses the different ways to offer a guitar for sale.

You can read those options without choosing one now. The useful next step may be identification, an appropriate appraisal or another family conversation. A sale is only one possible outcome.

If You're Not Ready To Decide

Knowing what the guitar is doesn't oblige you to sell it. Some families keep an instrument to play, some keep it as a family object, and some let it go after they have had time to think. A market opinion can stay in the file while that decision remains open.

Ask for the information the current situation needs and stop there if that is enough. You don't need to turn every question about an inherited guitar into a transaction. I'd rather you make a decision you can live with than hurry because you have finally learned the model name.

Questions About An Inherited Guitar

Can I Sell A Guitar Before Probate Is Finished?

That depends on ownership, the estate's authority and the applicable rules. Ask the executor or estate's attorney who can authorize a sale and when. Possessing the guitar or being a beneficiary doesn't settle that question.

What's The Difference Between An Appraisal And An Offer?

An appraisal gives an opinion of value for a stated purpose and date. An offer is a proposed transaction from a particular buyer. Check what each assumes before treating them as equivalent.

Do I Need A Written Appraisal For The Estate?

Ask the executor or professional requesting the valuation what documentation is needed. My free market opinion is informal; a signed report for official use is a separate paid service whose scope should be agreed first.

How Do I Find Out What My Father's Guitar Is Worth?

Begin with its identification, present condition and the purpose of the valuation. You can request an initial opinion without committing to sell, then establish whether the estate needs a different form of report.

What If We Don't Know What It Is?

You can ask for identification before deciding anything about value or a sale. Share what the family knows and leave uncertain details open instead of guessing from a similar-looking listing.

Should We Keep The Case And Papers With It?

Yes. The case, receipts, repair records, tags and family photographs can help document the instrument. Keep their connection clear, and discuss any private information before sharing it publicly.

Almost never: get the guitar assessed before restoring it for a sale. A full refinish generally means 40 to 60 percent less than a comparable original, even when the new surface looks better to its owner.

A buyer may prefer the worn original guitar you already have. Work done before an appraisal can remove material, spend money that won't be recovered or make its history harder to establish. Necessary structural work is a separate question from making it look ready to sell.

The guide to repairs and changes that affect value explains the broader comparisons. Here, the question is whether paying for work before a sale is likely to help you.

A Cleaner Appearance Can Leave Less Original Guitar

Restoration is a broad word. One shop might mean making an instrument safely playable; another might mean new finish, new hardware and an appearance closer to new. Before agreeing to the word, establish the specific work it includes.

Refinishing replaces or covers the existing protective surface. A full refinish generally means 40 to 60 percent less than a comparable original. That is why I don't recommend repainting a vintage guitar simply because the finish is worn, checked or dull.

The refinish value comparison explains the difference between full and partial work. An original surface can't be recovered just by choosing a historically appropriate paint color later. Preserving what survives gives the next owner a choice.

Polishing can also be more than a cosmetic change. Abrasive treatment can remove finish or plating, and cleaners can affect unfamiliar materials. Dust doesn't stop an initial appraisal, so making the guitar shine isn't a prerequisite for getting useful information.

Keep labels and old shop stickers attached. They can be part of the instrument's history, and lifting them can damage the surface underneath. Photographing them is enough for the first assessment.

Maintenance Still Needs A Purpose

A refret replaces the metal frets on the neck. A quality job is accepted maintenance and generally means 5 to 10 percent less than a comparable original. That doesn't mean you should pay for one before discovering whether the frets need replacement or whether a buyer wants to choose the work.

A neck reset restores an acoustic guitar's neck angle. Properly done, it carries almost no penalty. But the fact that a repair is appropriate doesn't establish that the seller should commission it before a sale.

A buyer may have a trusted repairer or a preferred setup. The waiting time and scope of the work can also affect your decision. Ask for an assessment of the current guitar before assuming that a repair bill will come back to you through the selling price.

Electronics deserve the same care. A crackling control, a faulty switch and a missing original pickup are different problems. A general request to replace anything old can remove components that a buyer would have preferred to keep or repair.

Describe the symptom instead of prescribing the fix. Saying that a control makes noise gives a repairer something to investigate. Saying that all the pots need replacing makes a decision before the circuit has been assessed.

What To Leave Alone For Now

Keep a stable guitar in its case in a suitable indoor room, and leave the surface, strings and parts alone until it has been assessed. Don't polish, oil, restring or adjust it to prepare for photographs. None of those jobs is required to identify the instrument.

If you have just found it after years in storage, follow the first steps for an old guitar for care, handling and photographs. That guide covers the practical details without turning an identification request into a repair project.

Keep loose parts and invoices with the guitar. A worn bridge, an old set of tuners or a bag of pickups can explain earlier work. Don't throw them away because the replacement parts are newer or shinier.

If work has already been done, there is no need to hide it or apologize for it. Tell the assessor what changed and provide the paperwork you have. The next useful step is to establish the guitar's present condition.

When Waiting Could Allow More Damage

An instrument being actively damaged needs handling advice rather than a routine instruction to leave everything untouched. A lifting acoustic bridge under string tension is one example. If you can safely loosen the strings without moving or stressing the damaged area, relieve that tension and ask a qualified repairer for advice.

Don't push the bridge back down, test the gap with a tool or add glue. If the tuners are stuck or handling looks unsafe, stop and ask before doing more. Stabilizing a problem and undertaking a full restoration are different decisions.

A visible crack or loose joint also needs to be assessed for stability. A photograph may establish that something needs attention without showing the full repair required. Explain what you can see and avoid flexing the instrument to demonstrate it.

If You Are Keeping It To Play

Keeping a guitar changes the goal. A useful repair or setup can make it enjoyable to play, and that can be a good reason to pay for work even if it doesn't increase resale value. Be clear with the repairer that you want original material preserved wherever practical.

Ask for the proposed work in ordinary language. Find out which parts would be adjusted, repaired or replaced, and ask for removed parts to be retained. That makes the choice concrete instead of relying on a broad promise to bring the guitar back.

A playable family instrument doesn't have to look new. Wear can stay while mechanical problems are addressed. Once its identity and condition are understood, you can choose the work that serves how you intend to use it.

Photographs And An Assessment Come First

Start with the guitar as it is, your reason for asking and whatever repair history you have. If you're deciding between an as-is sale and having work done, say that. The appraisal can then address the decision you actually face.

Questions About Work Before A Sale

Should I Clean Or Restring The Guitar Before An Appraisal?

No. Leave the surface, strings and parts as they are for the initial assessment. Clear photographs and an accurate description are more useful than cosmetic preparation.

Will A New Finish Help It Sell For More?

A full refinish generally means 40 to 60 percent less than a comparable original. Don't repaint a vintage guitar to prepare it for sale before its current finish and condition have been assessed.

Should I Pay For A Neck Reset Before Selling?

Get an assessment first. A proper reset carries almost no penalty, but you still need to compare the actual repair quote with the realistic sale options for the guitar as it stands.

What If The Bridge Is Lifting?

Ask for handling advice promptly. If you can safely loosen the strings without stressing the damaged area, relieve the tension. Don't press the bridge down, test the gap or apply glue; stop if handling or the tuners appear unsafe.

What If I Already Had The Guitar Restored?

Keep the invoices and removed parts, and describe the work plainly. An assessment can establish the instrument's current condition and the right comparison without pretending the changes never happened.

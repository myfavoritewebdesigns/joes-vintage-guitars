Names, Logos And Other Clues

What Brand Is
My Guitar?

The brand name is almost always on the front of the headstock, the widened end of the neck where the string tuning keys sit. If it isn't there, a label inside the body, the neck plate, the tuners and other fitted parts can help narrow it down.

Start by photographing what you can see, even if the lettering means nothing to you. I look at the name alongside the rest of the instrument, because a logo, a replacement part or a case can each tell a different story. You don't need to play guitar to collect those clues.

Look At The Headstock First

Support the guitar and look at the front of the headstock, above the long neck. The brand might be written across the top, arranged vertically or placed on a small cover. Photograph the entire outline first, then move closer for a second picture of the lettering.

Don't assume the largest word is the maker's name. A guitar can carry a brand, a model name and decorative wording in the same small area. Copy every readable word, keeping its spelling and punctuation, rather than choosing the one that looks most familiar.

Pearl Inlay. Pale, reflective letters may be separate pieces fitted into the surface. They can catch the light differently from the dark wood around them. Move the light or your phone slightly if glare hides part of a letter.

Decal Lettering. A decal is a thin transferred design, often with a fine outline around the letters. Its edges may be hard to distinguish beneath a clear finish. Don't rub it to find out whether it sits above or below the surface.

Printed Lettering. A silkscreen is a design printed onto the surface through a screen. It can look like flat painted writing rather than separate reflective pieces. A photograph is enough to start; you don't need to identify the printing method yourself.

Raised Logos And Stamps. A raised plastic name sits above the surface, while a stamp presses letters into the wood. Check the back as well as the front, since a maker's mark may be there. Leave any loose letters, badges or covers attached.

If the name reads Gibson, compare the lettering with Gibson's headstock logo by era. For a Martin, the guide to Martin's headstock logo shows how different marks can belong to the same brand. Neither comparison makes a logo alone proof that the whole guitar is original.

If There's No Name On The Headstock

A blank headstock doesn't end the search. I want photographs of the other places a name or identifying mark could survive, with enough of the surrounding guitar to show where each mark sits. You don't need to dismantle anything to make a useful first set.

Inside The Soundhole. An acoustic guitar has a hollow body that helps produce its sound. Look through the soundhole, the opening beneath the strings, for a paper label on the inside back. Photograph the whole label, including small print and handwriting, without touching it or trying to flatten a curled edge.

Some hollow guitars have narrow openings shaped like the letter f instead of a round hole. A label may be visible through one of them. Hold a light outside the opening and try another camera angle rather than putting tools inside.

The Neck Block. This is the thick piece of wood inside the body where the neck joins it. Look through the soundhole toward the neck for stamped words or numbers. Take one close photograph and one wider view; a number without its location is harder to interpret.

The Neck Plate. On some electric guitars, the neck is fastened to the body with screws through a metal plate on the back. Show the complete plate, its screws and any writing or numbers, plus the joint around it. A plain plate is still worth photographing, and you shouldn't loosen it to look underneath.

The Tuner Housings. Tuners are the mechanisms that tighten or loosen each string. Their housings are the metal covers or plates around the gears, usually visible on the back of the headstock. Photograph all of them together and any stamped name closely; that name may identify the parts supplier rather than the guitar brand.

Inside A Control Cavity. An electric guitar may have a compartment for the wiring beneath its knobs and switches. A stamp inside can be useful if you already have a photograph from a repair or inspection. Don't remove a cover or lift the wiring just to search; send the outside views first.

The Case Badge. Photograph any name on the case, then the lining and the way the guitar fits inside. The badge might name the guitar brand, a shop or the case maker. Cases get swapped, so I treat that name as supporting information rather than an answer by itself.

Keep the photographs together and note where each mark appears. If two names disagree, include both instead of trying to decide which one belongs. The difference could reflect a replaced part, a different case or the way the instrument was originally sold.

When The Name On It Isn't Who Built It

Some guitars were sold under a name owned by a store, a catalog business or a distributor. These are often called house brands or store brands. The name tells you how the guitar was marketed, while a separate factory may have built it.

Rebadged imports fit the same general picture: an instrument made elsewhere carries the seller's chosen name. That doesn't make the name false or the guitar worthless. It means identifying the name on the instrument and identifying the factory are two different tasks.

I compare the label wording, construction and fitted parts before connecting an unfamiliar name to a builder. Similar body shapes or matching tuners aren't enough to establish a shared factory. If the evidence supports only the selling brand, that's the identification I'll give you.

A Worn Or Missing Logo

Look for the outline of former lettering, small mounting holes or a faint patch where a decal once sat. Photograph that area straight on and again with light coming from the side. Don't scrape, polish or wet the surface to make it clearer; those steps can remove the traces you're trying to record.

Include the full headstock shape, the positions of the tuners and the place where the neck meets the body. Those features help me compare possibilities even when I can't read a name. A familiar outline is a clue, though copies and replacement necks can share it.

A blank face doesn't tell you why the name is missing. It might never have carried a front logo, or its surface may have been altered later. I need the back of the headstock and the rest of the guitar before drawing that distinction.

FAQ

There's No Name Anywhere On My Guitar. Can It Still Be Identified?

Often, yes. The headstock shape, body construction, fitted parts and any remaining stamps can narrow the possibilities. Send photographs of the whole guitar and both sides of the headstock; I may be able to identify it or explain what evidence is still missing.

The Logo Is Worn Off. Is There Another Way?

Look for an interior label, a maker's stamp or identifying marks on the back of the instrument. Include any faint lettering or empty mounting holes in your photographs. Don't try to restore or redraw the logo before the guitar has been assessed.

Is A Guitar With No Brand Name Worth Anything?

It can be, but the missing name doesn't establish value either way. The maker, model, condition and original parts all need consideration. I wouldn't discard a guitar simply because its headstock is blank.

Where's The Label On An Acoustic?

Look through the soundhole at the inside back of the body, then toward the neck block where the neck joins the body. Some guitars have stamped marks rather than a paper label. If you can't see one, photograph what's visible without removing strings or reaching inside.

Does The Name On The Tuners Tell Me The Guitar Brand?

Sometimes it helps, but it may be the name of the company that made the tuners. Those parts can also have been replaced. I compare their markings with the guitar's other details before using them to support an identification.

Can I Identify It From The Case Name?

The case name is a clue, but the guitar may have been put in a different case. Photograph both and keep any paperwork or loose parts with them. I won't use a case badge alone to assign the guitar a brand.

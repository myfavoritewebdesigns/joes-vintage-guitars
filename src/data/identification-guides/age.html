Find The Serial Number First

Start with the places you can see while the guitar is safely supported. Photograph the complete number, then take a wider view showing where it appears. Include every letter and any wording beside it, even if you think it isn't part of the number.

The Headstock. This is the widened end of the neck where the string tuning keys sit. Check its back and front for numbers stamped into the wood, printed on the surface or carried on a label. Don't rub a faint stamp or scrape the finish to make it clearer.

Inside An Acoustic. An acoustic guitar has a hollow body that helps produce its sound. Look through the soundhole, the opening under the strings, for a paper label or a stamp on the wood. Narrow openings shaped like the letter f can also provide a view of an interior label.

The Neck Plate. Some electric guitars have a metal plate on the back where screws fasten the neck to the body. The number may be stamped into that plate. Photograph the screws and surrounding area as well as the digits, without loosening anything.

The Neck Heel. The heel is the thicker end of the neck where it meets the body. Marks may appear along it or on its flat end, sometimes hidden inside the joint. Those marks might be production dates or workshop codes rather than the guitar's serial, so keep their location with the photograph.

Inside A Control Cavity. An electric guitar may have numbers or stamps in the compartment beneath its knobs and switches. If you already have inspection photographs, include them. Leave removing covers, lifting wiring or separating the neck from the body to someone familiar with vintage instruments.

Write down uncertain characters as uncertain rather than filling them in from a search result. Try a photograph with the light coming from another direction if glare hides a digit. Keep the original image alongside anything you've cropped, so I can see the surrounding details.

The Serial Tells You Less Than You'd Think

A lookup can offer a plausible year without establishing that it's the right year for your guitar. Manufacturers have reused numbers, run overlapping systems and applied different numbering practices to different instruments. The brand, model and location of the number determine which chart is relevant.

My explanation of what a serial number can't prove shows why I check the instrument beyond its digits. A readable number doesn't establish original finish, original parts or an unchanged neck. It also doesn't prove that the number was applied at the factory.

Numbers can be restamped, and a numbered neck plate or replacement neck can move from one guitar to another. That doesn't tell you why the work happened. It means I need other evidence before treating the number as a record of the instrument in front of me.

A date found on a component may record when that part was made rather than when the guitar was assembled. Even a correctly read factory marking can answer a narrower question than the one you're asking. I separate the serial result, the part dates and the construction clues before deciding how closely they agree.

If two websites return different years, don't choose the older answer because it seems more desirable. Keep both results and check which system each site assumed. A clear photograph of the number and its location can explain a disagreement that another search won't resolve.

The Parts Date The Guitar Too

A guitar can contain parts made at different times. Factories could hold stock before using it, and owners could replace a worn or broken component later. I need to establish which parts belong to the original assembly before using their dates to narrow the guitar's age.

The guitar couldn't have been assembled before its latest original component was made. That gives a boundary, not an exact assembly date. A later replacement doesn't make the entire guitar newer, and an older replacement doesn't make it older.

Volume And Tone Controls. A potentiometer, usually shortened to pot, is the electrical control beneath a volume or tone knob. A pot code is the maker's marking on that component, which may identify when it was manufactured. The guides to Fender pot codes and Gibson pot codes explain how to read that evidence without treating it as the guitar's birthday.

Neck And Body Markings. Written or stamped production marks can date stages in making the neck or body. My guides to Fender neck dates and Fender body dates explain what those marks refer to. Their agreement is more useful when the neck and body also appear to belong together.

Pickups. These are the parts beneath an electric guitar's strings that turn string movement into an electrical signal. Some have dated markings, while others need comparison by construction and materials. The Fender pickup dates guide explains the evidence, including why a pickup's age doesn't by itself date the instrument carrying it.

Tuners And Knobs. Tuners are the mechanisms you turn to tighten or loosen the strings. They don't all carry readable dates, so their design and markings can help establish a period instead. Use Gibson tuner dating, Martin tuner dating and Gibson knob dating to compare the relevant parts rather than expecting a date stamped on every piece.

Construction And Interior Stamps. The guides to Gibson parts and hardware and Fender physical features bring several clues together. For an acoustic, Martin neck block stamps explains marks on the thick interior wood where the neck joins the body. A model designation, a serial and a maker's stamp serve different purposes even when they're close together.

When The Serial And The Parts Disagree

A mismatch is information about the guitar's history. A replaced control, pickup or tuner could reflect ordinary repair work, and a guitar with replacement parts may still have a clear identity. I describe the change separately from the age of the original instrument.

Several conflicting clues need a closer look. The possibilities include a mistaken reading, a later replacement neck, a guitar assembled from separate parts or an altered identity. I won't call it a fake simply because one component is different, and I won't ignore the difference because the serial gives a convenient answer.

My guides to spotting a fake Gibson, spotting a fake Fender and spotting a fake Martin explain the wider checks. Keep any repair invoices or photographs of earlier work with the guitar. That history may help explain a feature that otherwise looks out of place.

FAQ

Where's The Serial Number On An Acoustic Guitar?

Look through the soundhole for a paper label or a stamp on the neck block, the thick wood inside the body where the neck joins it. Also check the back of the headstock. You don't need to remove the strings or reach inside to begin photographing what's visible.

My Serial Number Returns Two Different Years. Which Is Right?

The number alone may not settle it. Check the brand, model and number's location, then compare the parts and construction with the possible dates. I keep both possibilities open until another clue supports one of them.

There's No Serial Number At All. Can It Still Be Dated?

Often it can be placed within a period from its construction, markings and original parts. Some guitars didn't receive a serial, and others have lost their identifying marks. An exact year may remain uncertain even after a careful inspection.

Do The Dates On The Parts Have To Match?

No, original components can have been made at different times before the guitar was assembled. I check whether their dates and construction fit together, rather than expecting every part to match exactly. A replacement part needs to be considered separately.

Does The Year Change What It's Worth?

It can, but the model, condition, original parts and demand also affect value. Two guitars made in the same year can have very different histories and values. I establish what the instrument is before treating a year as a reason to assign a price.

Should I Take It Apart To Find More Dates?

No, start with the markings you can photograph without removing anything. Old screws, wiring and finishes can be damaged during disassembly. If hidden dates are needed, have someone familiar with vintage guitars carry out that inspection.

Can You Date It From Photos?

I can often identify a likely year or period from clear photographs of the guitar and its markings. Some questions about hidden parts or originality need a closer inspection. I'll tell you which conclusion the photographs support rather than promise an exact year from a serial alone.
